>[!definition] Ring Homomorphism
> A *ring homomorphism* is a map $\varphi \colon R \to S$ between [[Ring, Field and Integral Domain#^concept-b822d04b4a4b|rings]] which is compatible with addition, multiplication and maps the unit of $R$ to that of $S$ (if they are unital). A ring homomorphism which admits an inverse is an isomorphism. ^16be07

> [!proposition]
> For every ring $R$ there is a unique homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to R$.

*Proof*  We must have $φ(1) = 1$; and then $φ(n) = 1+\dots+1$ ($n$ ones) for $n ∈ \mathbb{N}$; and $φ(−n) = −φ(n)$. It is easy to check that this is multiplicative as well. $\square$

> [!definition] Characteristic
> Let $\newcommand{\Z}{\mathbb{Z}}\psi\colon \Z\to R$ be the unique ring homomorphism from the integers to a ring $R$. Then the characteristic of $R$ is the smallest nonnegative integer $n$ such that $\ker\psi=n\Z$. 
> i.e., $n$ is the smallest positive integer such that $n\cdot 1=0$ in $R$, or $n=0$ if no such integer exists. ^2bfbd8

<u><b>e.g.</b></u>  The characteristic of $\Z$ is $0$, the characteristic of $\Z/n\Z$ is $n$.

> [!proposition]
> $R[x,y] \cong R[x][y]$.

> [!definition] Kernel and Image
> The *kernel* and *image* of a ring homomorphism $φ \colon R \to S$ are
> $$\ker \varphi = \{ r\in R \mid \varphi(r)=0 \},\quad \operatorname{im}\varphi = \{s\in S \mid \exists r\in R, \varphi(r)=s \}.$$
> 

> [!remark]
> The image is a subring of $S$ because $φ(a) − φ(b) = φ(a − b)$, $φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b)$, $1 = φ(1)$. The kernel is an *ideal* of $R$, which is defined in the next section.
> 

## Ideals

> [!definition] Ideal
> A *left ideal* in a ring $R$ is a subset $I$ of $R$ such that 
> - $0\in R$;
> - $I$ is closed under addition;
> - If $a\in I$ then $ra\in I$ for all $r\in R$.
>
> Equivalently, it is a non-empty subset such that for $a_1 ,\dots,a_k ∈I$ we have $r_1 a_1 +\dots+r_k a_k ∈I$ for every $r_1,\dots,r_k ∈R$. We write $I\triangleleft R$ in such case. ^395472

> [!remark]+ Ideals to Rings are Normal Subgroups to Groups
> Ideals are the ring-theoretic analogue of [[Homomorphisms, Normal Subgroup & Conjugation#^normal|normal subgroups]] in group theory. Just as normal subgroups allow us to form quotient groups, ideals allow us to form quotient rings.

> [!definition] Generated Ideal
> Let $a_1,\dots,a_n ∈ R$. The set $\{ r_1a_1+\dots r_n a_n \mid r_i\in \mathbb{R}\}$ is an ideal in $R$ called the ideal generated by $a_1,\dots,a_n$. The notation is $I = (a_1,a_2\dots,a_n)$.
> An ideal $I$ is *principal* if it is generated by one element. That is, there exists $a ∈ R$ such that $I = \{ra \mid r ∈ R\}$. It is denoted $(a) = Ra = aR$. ^027c69

<u><b>e.g.</b></u>  In $\mathbb{Z}$ every ideal is principal and takes the form $n\mathbb{Z} = (n)$. (ref. [[Principle Ideal Domains and Various Ideals#^a3b745|proposition]]).

> [!definition] Product Ideal
> For $I,J\triangleleft R$, define the product ideal $IJ$ as $$IJ=\left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i \mid n\in \mathbb{N}, x_i\in I,y_i\in J  \right\}.$$

> [!proposition]
> If $I,J\triangleleft R$ are ideals so are $I\cap J$ and $IJ$.

*Proof*  It is clear that $I\cap J$ is an ideal. For $IJ$, we have $0\in IJ$ since $0\in I,J$. If $x=\sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i$ and $y=\sum_{j=1}^m u_jv_j$ are in $IJ$, then $x+y=\sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i+\sum_{j=1}^m u_jv_j\in IJ$. If $r\in R$, then $rx=\sum_{i=1}^n (rx_i)y_i\in IJ$. $\square$

## Quotient rings

> [!definition] Quotient Ring
> Let $R$ be a ring and $I$ an ideal. Then, as an additive subgroup, $I$ is [[Homomorphisms, Normal Subgroup & Conjugation#^normal|normal]] in $R$ and we can form the quotient $$ R/I=\{ a+I \mid a\in R \} $$with addition $(a + I ) + (b + I ) = a + b + I$ , zero $I$ , and inverse $−(a + I ) = −a + I$. There is a unique way to upgrade this abelian group to a ring, called the *quotient ring*. Define $$ (a+I)\cdot(b+I):=ab+I. $$

We interpret the quotient $R/(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ as imposing the relations $a_1=a_2=\dots=a_n=0$ or ‘killing’ the elements $a_i$. Killing the elements $a_i$ in installments and in various orders, or in one strike lead to the same result. That is, we have following commutative diagram:
<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yiran-frank-mao/image_repo/master/Obsidian/imposing_relations.svg" alt="imposing_relations" style="width:25%;">
A primary tool for constructing new rings is to take a ring $R$ and a ‘free’ variable and create the polynomial ring, and then quotient by a polynomial. We will see this further in [[Fields Construction#Adjoining Roots|field extensions by adjoining roots of polynomials.]]

<u><b>e.g.</b></u>  
- If $f$ in $R[x]$ has no root in $R[x]$ we can form the quotient $R[x]/\left(f (x)\right)$ and $\overline{x} = x +(f (x))$ is a root of $f$.
- The complex numbers are constructed from $\R$ by setting $\mathbb{C} := \R[x]/(x^2+1)$. In fact, we can define $\varphi : \mathbb{C} \to \R[x]/(x^2+1), a+bi \mapsto \overline{a+bx}$. Clearly it is surjective and injective. Since $$ \begin{align}
\varphi(a+bi)\varphi(c+di)&=\overline{ac+(ad+bc)x+bdx^2} \\
&=\overline{(ad+bc)x-bd+ac} \\
&=\varphi((ad+bc)i+(ac-bd)) \\
&=\varphi((a+bi)(c+di)),
\end{align} $$$\varphi$ is homomorphism, yielding a isomorphism. ^a4b259
$\quad$

> [!proposition] Universal Property of Quotients
> Let $\varphi\colon R\to S$ be a ring homomorphism with kernel $K$ and let $I$ be another ideal. Let $π\colon R\to R/I$ be the canonical quotient map. Then if $I \subset K$,there exists a unique ring homomorphism $\bar{\varphi}:R/I →S$ such that $φ=\bar{\varphi}\circ \pi$.
> 

> [!theorem] First Isomorphism Theorem
> Let $\varphi \colon R\to S$ be a ring homomorphism with kernel $K$. If $φ$ is surjective then $\bar{φ}\colon R/K →S$ is an isomorphism.

<u><b>e.g.</b></u>  $\R[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ can also be showed using the first isomorphism theorem.

> [!theorem] Correspondence Theorem
> Let $φ \colon R → R^\prime$ be a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $K$. There is a bijective correspondence:
> $$\begin{aligned}\{\text{Ideals of } R \text{ containing } K\} &\longleftrightarrow\{\text{Ideals of } R^\prime\} \\I &\shortmid\!\longrightarrow \varphi(I) \\I^\prime &\longleftarrow\!\shortmid \varphi^{-1}(I^\prime)\end{aligned} $$
> If the ideal $I$ corresponds to the ideal $I^\prime$ then $R/I \cong R^\prime/I^\prime$.

> [!corollary]
> Let $R$ be a ring, suppose $I \triangleleft R$. Then the ideals of $R/I$ corresponds to ideals of $R$ containing $I$.

## Chinese Remainder Theorem

> [!lemma]
> Suppose, $I,J\triangleleft R$, then $R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J$. ^2e52b1

*Proof*  Define $\varphi\colon R \to R/I \times R/J$ by $\varphi(r) = (r + I, r + J)$. It is easy to check that $\varphi$ is a ring homomorphism (actually, it is the product of two quotient maps). The kernel of $\varphi$ is $I \cap J$, and the image is all of $R/I \times R/J$. By the first isomorphism theorem, we have $R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J$. $\square$

> [!theorem] Chinese Remainder Theorem
> Let $I, J \triangleleft R$ such that $I + J = R$, then $I J =I \cap J$ and $R/I J\cong R/I ×R/J$.  ^27e653

*Proof*  We first show that $IJ=I\cap J$. It is clear that $IJ\subseteq I\cap J$. Conversely, for all $a\in I\cap J$, note that since $R=I+J$, we have $1=x_{0}+y_{0}$ for some $x_{0}\in I,y_{0}\in J$. Then $a=x_{0}a + y_{0}a \in IJ$. Thus $I\cap J\subseteq IJ$. Therefore, combining [[Ring Homomorphisms and Ideals#^2e52b1|the above lemma]], we have $R/IJ\cong R/I\times R/J$. $\square$

> [!remark]
> Given $I + J = R$, $IJ=I\cap J$ requires $R$ being a commutative unital ring, while $R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J$ holds for any ring $R$.
>
