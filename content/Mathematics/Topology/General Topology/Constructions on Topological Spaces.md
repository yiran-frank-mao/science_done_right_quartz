## Subspaces

>[!definition] Subspace Topology
>If $(T,\mathcal{T})$ is a topological space and $S\subset T$ , then the subspace topology on $S$ is $$\mathcal{T}_{S}=\{U\cap S \mid U \in \mathcal{T} \}$$ We call $(S,\mathcal{T}_{S})$ a topological subspace of $T$. ^a942da

<b><u>e.g.</u></b>  If we consider $[0,1]$ as a subspace of $\R$ then the open sets in $[0,1]$ consist of all sets $U\cap [0,1]$ where $U$ is an open subset of $\R$. In particular, $[0, a)$ is open in the subspace for every $a\in(0, 1)$.

> Suppose that $(X, d)$ is a metric space with corresponding topology $\mathcal{T}$. If $S\subset X$ then subspace topology $\mathcal{T}_{S}$ on $S$ corresponds to the topology on $S$ that arises from the metric space $(S,d\big|_{S})$.

>[!proposition] 
> Closed subset in closed subspace is closed in the original topological space.

*Proof*  Let $S$ be a closed subspace of $X$, and $C\subset S$ is closed in $S$. Then $$S\setminus C = W \cap S$$for some open set $W$ in $X$. It follows that $C = S\setminus(W\cap S)=S\setminus W$. Thus, $$X\setminus C = X \setminus (S\setminus W)=(X\setminus S) \cup W$$Since $S$ is closed in $X$, $X\setminus S$ is open. Therefore $X\setminus C$ is open, and $C$ is closed in $X$. $\square$

> [!theorem] Universal Property of the Subspace Topology
> Let $X$ be a topological space and $Y$ a subspace (with the subspace topology). Then
> 1. The inclusion map $\iota\colon Y\to X$ is continuous;
> 2. For any topological space $W$, a map $f \colon W \to Y$ is continuous iff the composite $\iota \circ f \colon W \to X$ is continuous;
> 3. The subspace topology is the unique topology on $Y$ with this property.
> 
> <img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yiran-frank-mao/image_repo/master/Obsidian/subspace_topology.svg" style="width:25%;"/>

*Proof*  The continuity of the inclusion map follows from 2. For 2, suppose $f\colon W\to Y$ is continuous, then for any open set $U\subset X$, we have $(\iota\circ f)^{-1}(U)=f^{-1}(\iota^{-1}(U))=f^{-1}(U\cap Y)$, which is open in $W$. The converse is similar. To show such topology is unique, suppose $\mathcal{T}'$ is another topology on $Y$ with the same property. Then the identity map $\newcommand{\id}{\operatorname{id}}\id\colon (Y,\mathcal{T}')\to (Y,\mathcal{T})$ is continuous. Thus for any open set $U\in \mathcal{T}$, we have $\id^{-1}(U)=U\in \mathcal{T}'$. Similarly, we can show that $\mathcal{T}'\subset \mathcal{T}$. Therefore, $\mathcal{T}'=\mathcal{T}$. $\square$

> [!corollary]
> Suppose $f\colon X\to Y$ is continuous. Then
> - The restriction $f\big|_{S}\colon S\to Y$ is continuous for any subspace $S\subset X$;
> - If $W$ is a subspace of $Y$ that contains the image $f (X)$, then $f \colon X \to W$ is continuous;
> - If $Y$ is a subspace of $Z$, then $f \colon X \to Z$ is continuous.
> $\quad$

*Proof*  This is obvious from the above theorem. $\square$

## Product Spaces

>[!definition] Product Topology
> Suppose that $(X_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i})_{i\in I}$ is a family of [[Mathematics/Topology/General Topology/Topological Spaces#^concept-39ce12df888c|topological spaces]]. Then the *product topology* on their Cartesian product $\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}$ is the topology $\mathcal{T}$ with [[Topological Spaces#^2fc468|basis]] $$\left\{ \prod_{i\in I} U_{i} \mid U_{i}\in \mathcal{T}_{i} \text{ and } U_{i}=X_{i} \text{ for all but finitely many }i \right\}.$$ 
> We call $\mathcal{T}$ the *product topology* on $\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}$. ^fbf303

> [!remark]-
> Note that $\mathcal{T}_{1}\times \mathcal{T}_{2}$ may not be a topology on $T_{1}\times T_{2}$. For example, on $\R^{2}$, since every open set in $\R$ is a disjoint union of open intervals, sets in the Cartesian product of the standard topologies are disjoint union of rectangles. But clearly, the union of two rectangles may not be a rectangle. 
> <img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yiran-frank-mao/image_repo/master/Obsidian/cartesian_product_of_topology.svg" style="width:32%;"/>
> Moreover, the product topology is independent of choice of the bases. This is because if $\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{A}'$ and $\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{B}'$ are bases for $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{S}$, respectively, then the topology generated by $\mathcal{A}\times \mathcal{B}$ is the same as the topology generated by $\mathcal{A}'\times \mathcal{B}'$. (This is a direct result of [[Construction of Sets#^9d651c|proposition]])

> [!lemma]
> The product topology is generated by the [[Topological Spaces#^02668a|subbasis]] $$\mathcal{S}=\{\pi_i^{-1}(U_i):U_i\in \mathcal{T}_i\},$$ where $\pi_j\colon \prod_{i\in I}X_{i}\to X_{j}$ is the projection map. In particular, it is the coarsest topology on $\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}$ such that all projection maps $\pi_{j}\colon \prod_{i\in I}X_{i}\to X_{j}$ are continuous.

*Proof*  Suppose $\prod_{i\in I} U_{i}$ is an open set in the basis of the product topology, and $J\subset I$ is the finite set of indices for which $U_{j}\neq X_{j}$. Then we have $$\prod_{i\in I} U_{i}=\bigcap_{j\in J}\pi_j^{-1}(U_j)$$which is a finite intersection of sets in $\mathcal{S}$. $\square$

> [!theorem] Universal Property of Product Topology
> The product topological space is the [[Products of Objects#^728bf5|product]] in the category $\mathsf{Top}$, i.e. for any [[Mathematics/Topology/General Topology/Topological Spaces#^concept-39ce12df888c|topological space]] $Z$ and a family of continuous maps $f_{i}\colon Z\to X_{i}$, there exists a unique continuous map $f\colon Z\to \prod_{i\in I}X_{i}$ such that $\pi_{i}\circ f=f_{i}$ for all $i\in I$, where $\pi_{j}\colon \prod_{i\in I}X_{i}\to X_{j}$ is the projection map.
> <img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yiran-frank-mao/image_repo/master/Obsidian/product_topology.svg" style="width:32%;"/>
> Moreover, $\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}$ is the unique space with this property, up to homeomorphism.

*Proof*  We will show that each $f_j$ is continuous iff $f$ is continuous. Suppose $f$ is continuous, then for any open set $U_j\subset X_j$, we have $$f_j^{-1}(U_j)=(\pi_j\circ f)^{-1}(U_j)=f^{-1}(\pi_j^{-1}(U_j))$$Since $\pi_j^{-1}(U_j)$ is open in $\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}$ (by definition of the product topology), we have $f_j^{-1}(U_j)$ is open in $Z$. Conversely, suppose each $f_j$ is continuous. Since the product topology is generated by the $\mathcal{S}=\{\pi_i^{-1}(U_i):U_i\in \mathcal{T}_i\}$, it suffices to show that $f^{-1}(S)$ is open in $Z$ for any $S\in \mathcal{S}$. For any $j\in I$ and any open set $U_j\subset X_j$, we have $$f^{-1}(\pi_j^{-1}(U_j))=(\pi_j\circ f)^{-1}(U_j)=f_j^{-1}(U_j)$$which is open in $Z$. Therefore, $f$ is continuous. $\square$

## Disjoint Union Topology

> [!definition] Disjoint Union Topology
> Suppose that $(X_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i})_{i\in I}$ is a family of [[Mathematics/Topology/General Topology/Topological Spaces#^concept-39ce12df888c|topological spaces]]. Then the *disjoint union topology* or *corproduct topology* on their disjoint union $\bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}$ is defined by declaring that $U\subset \bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}$ is open iff $U\cap X_{i}$ is open in $X_{i}$ for each $i\in I$. ^e08e6f

> [!theorem] Universal Property of Disjoint Union Topology
> The disjoint union of topological spaces is the [[Products of Objects#^e08759|coproduct]] in the category $\mathsf{Top}$, i.e. for any [[Mathematics/Topology/General Topology/Topological Spaces#^concept-39ce12df888c|topological space]] $Z$ and a family of continuous maps $f_{i}\colon X_{i}\to Z$, there exists a unique continuous map $f\colon \bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}\to Z$ such that $f\circ \iota_i=f_{i}$ for all $i\in I$, where $\iota_j\colon X_j\to \bigsqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}$ is the inclusion map.

## Quotient Topology

> [!definition] Quotient Topology
> Suppose $X$ is a topological space and $Y$ is a set. $q\colon X\to Y$ is surjective, then the *quotient toplogy* is a topology on $Y$ by declaring that $U\subset Y$ is open iff $q^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$. ^d57887
